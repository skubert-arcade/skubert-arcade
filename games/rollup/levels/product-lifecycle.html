Product lifecycle

From a locally hosted, adapted encyclopedia-style article

About this page: This is a shortened, rewritten game adaptation of the Wikipedia article. It is not a live Wikipedia page, and article text may differ from the original.

In industry, product lifecycle management (PLM) concerns the information, people, and processes involved in bringing a product into existence and supporting it throughout its useful life. A lifecycle may begin with a concept, proceed through design and production, and continue with maintenance, updates, retirement, and disposal.

Product information is often managed by several connected systems. Engineering software describes what should be made. Manufacturing systems organize how it is produced. Business applications support procurement, inventory, orders, and distribution. Across these activities, lifecycle management helps teams share dependable product records.

History

Computer-aided design and product data management helped establish modern lifecycle management. As companies created increasingly complex products, they needed better ways to retain drawings, specifications, revisions, and decisions. Centralized records reduced the need to reconcile disconnected copies of the same technical information.

In the automotive industry, design teams began connecting engineering documentation with production and business operations. These approaches influenced the broader development of coordinated product-data workflows.

Forms

Product lifecycle management is distinct from the marketing concept of a product's sales lifecycle. Engineering-oriented PLM concentrates on the design, manufacture, maintenance, and retirement of a product. Commercial lifecycle analysis typically examines introduction, growth, maturity, and decline in the market.

Organizations may combine project planning, technical documentation, product definition, supplier collaboration, configuration management, and change control. Some organizations also incorporate feedback from products already in service to help guide future designs.

Benefits

  • More consistent access to current product data.
  • Better coordination between engineering and manufacturing.
  • Reduced duplication of drawings, specifications, and revisions.
  • Clearer understanding of changes and approvals.
  • Improved support for service, repair, and eventual retirement.
  • Greater visibility into development schedules and dependencies.

Overview of product lifecycle management

PLM may connect systems engineering, portfolio planning, product design, manufacturing process management, and product data management. Each discipline focuses on a different decision, but they rely on related information about the same products.

For example, a change to a component dimension might affect a drawing, an approved bill of materials, manufacturing instructions, a supplier order, and a maintenance manual. Coordinated product data helps teams identify which records need review.

Phases of the product lifecycle

Conceive

Teams collect requirements, explore alternatives, estimate costs, and decide which problems the proposed product should solve. Early concepts may include sketches, preliminary specifications, and expected customer needs.

Design

Design work turns a concept into technical definitions that can be tested and refined. Drawings, models, materials, prototypes, analyses, and validation evidence help teams evaluate whether requirements are being met.

Realize

The realization phase prepares and executes manufacturing, purchasing, assembly, testing, and delivery. Product records must connect what was designed with the actual parts and processes used to produce it.

Service

Once delivered, products may require installation instructions, documentation, replacement parts, inspections, repairs, upgrades, and support. At the end of use, the lifecycle can include recycling, disposal, and retirement from the product portfolio.

Connected processes and information

Product information is most useful when it remains traceable across systems and departments. Engineering manages definitions and approved changes. Operations needs production instructions and accurate materials. Customer-facing channels need descriptions, media, specifications, and availability details.

For distributors, a related challenge is making sure the same item is recognized across product information management, warehouse systems, ecommerce sites, and digital asset libraries. That information-sharing problem is related to lifecycle coordination, even when the distributor is not responsible for engineering the product itself.
